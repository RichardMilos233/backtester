Regime count (A4/A5): relative US-vs-international wealth contains **4 switches of leader in 1990-2026** (FF, 25% reversal filter: US to 1993, international to 1994, US to 2002, international to 2008, US since) and **10 switches in 1980-2026** (VFINX/PRITX); but only two international-lead episodes lasted 3+ years (1985-88 and 2002-08; with VWIGX also 1999-2010 and 2016-21). The evidence therefore rests on roughly 2 long US-lead and 2 long international-lead regimes, not on 36 or 46 independent years. Every 20-year window contains at least one US-lead regime, which is why the US wins 100% of 20y windows in the 1990+ record; that is a statement about how the legs are arranged, not evidence about the next 20 years.

In plain words: in every dataset the historical price of holding 20% ex-US is about 0.4-0.8 pp/yr of 20y IRR; a cap-weighted world index (about 25% ex-US today) costs about 0.7-1.0 pp/yr on the same basis (the 2.7 figure is for the historical FF_DEV blend, not today's index). In a stipulated 2pp/yr US-underperformance scenario the same holdings gain about 0.4-0.5 pp/yr (10th-percentile gain 0.8-1.0). The intervals around both include zero (the FF "cost" has upper bounds of +0.1/+0.2), so neither sign is firmly separated from noise at 90%. The insurance does not shrink maximum drawdown in this record (0.7-2.4pp deeper).

The window beginning 1999-03 contains the 2000-2009 decade: the single non-overlapping 10y window gives S&P -8.2%, world -5.0%, S&P 70/BRK 30 -5.5%, world 70/BRK 30 -3.5%, so there world and Berkshire help (one observation). Berkshire is a known winner picked in hindsight: its +1.0pp and high P come from BRK-A's 1990-2026 outperformance (daily-mean annualised about 14.7% vs 12.1% for FF_MKT since 1993) and should not be extrapolated; its drawdown benefit is partly diversification (daily correlation about 0.5 with the S&P).

## What the evidence supports and what it does not

- The historical cost of any ex-US allocation is positive and modest for small shares (0.4-0.8 pp/yr at 20%, 0.7-1.0 for a cap-weighted world index at today's 25% ex-US) and 90% intervals reach zero.
- The evidence rests on 2-4 sustained regimes: US lead 1994-2002 and 2008/10-2026, international lead 1985-88 and 2002-08. Windows of 10-30 years always contain a US-lead leg, so "the US won every 20y window" describes the arrangement of legs in this one record.
- The bootstrap re-draws blocks of this one history and cannot generate an extended international-lead regime; the reversal scenario is imposed and its 2pp/yr size is an assumption.
- A world index did not reduce max drawdown in this record; it reduced the worst 10-year IRR (-4.7% vs -7.0%, FF) and nav volatility (14.7% vs 18.1%).
- Singapore withholding (about 0.6 pp/yr on US-listed S&P/world, 0.9 on ex-US) is of the same order as the cost of a small ex-US allocation; an Irish accumulating line recovers about 0.3-0.6 of it.

## Deviations, assumptions, data quirks

- FF_DEV is used as the "MSCI-World-like" series: developed markets only, implied ex-US share ~54% over 1990-2026 (40% US in the 1990s, 56% since 2010). The "MSCI World" cost is therefore shown both for FF_DEV and at today's weight (a constant mix of FF_MKT and FF_DEVXUS with 25.4% ex-US, from the Part C regression).
- Bootstrap IRR uses a monthly grid (deposit at month start, 5bp slippage, month-end valuation), not the engine's first-trading-day open; checked within 0.02pp against `rolling_dca`. Mixes are never rebalanced. 20y IRR = first 240 months of 480-month simulated paths.
- "No US edge" equates arithmetic monthly means; ex-US volatility is higher so its geometric return is slightly lower than the S&P's in that scenario.
- NET15x and the constant net-of-15% drags for 1990+ are approximations (E0); IWDA.L implied weights/tracking use 5-day returns; DCA on London lines (D2) uses London opens.
- XUS.SI / XWR.SI: 23 usable rows; correlations indicative only.
- No changes to `src/`; no bug found in `src/dca.py` in the code paths used.
